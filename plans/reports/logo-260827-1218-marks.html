The brief

What the current mark does and doesn't do.

Two circles, one filled. It reads as bubbles — pleasant, on-name, and that is all it says. Cover the wordmark and it could belong to a soap brand, a spa, or a fizzy drink. A mark that carried the product's actual idea — a private space with a gate that opens only when it matters — would be doing real work.

Why The Gate. It's the only one whose form comes from the product rather than from the name. The dashed rule already appears on the landing page and on the parent dashboard, so the logo stops being decoration and becomes the same sentence the product keeps saying: there is a line, and most of what your child says stays above it. Cover the wordmark and it still means something — the test the current mark fails.

Runner-up: Held. Warmer, and the deliberate gap in the arc is a real idea — care that isn't enclosure. Choose it if “gate” feels too much like a barrier for a children's product.

Reversed

One honest caveat. The dashes are the risk. At 16px they can blur into a solid rule on a low-DPI screen, which quietly loses the idea. Fix is a favicon-specific variant with a solid line and slightly heavier stroke — same shape, one optical size, which is normal practice.
